The Subconscious Mind: What It Is and How Patterns Change

By Lance AdhikariAugust 1, 202614 min readIndependent research

A visual guide to the mental processes that operate outside immediate awareness, how habits become automatic, and what it really means to change a background pattern.

Question I wanted to answer

Can I change my "subconscious" by changing what I repeatedly do?

02

My short answer is yes, partly. Repeated actions and experiences can influence habits, expectations, emotional associations, and automatic responses. But this is gradual learning, not the instant reprogramming of a separate hidden mind.

I hear the word subconscious used to explain almost everything: intuition, confidence, fear, habits, dreams, self-belief, and even success. That makes the word feel powerful, but also vague. Before asking how to change the subconscious, I first needed a clearer picture of what the term can reasonably mean.

In everyday language, the subconscious is a useful umbrella for mental activity that influences us without occupying the centre of attention. Modern psychology usually uses narrower terms such as automatic processing, implicit memory, implicit learning, habit, and nonconscious processing. These terms describe processes that researchers can define and test without claiming that a second mind is hiding inside the brain.

1. What is the subconscious mind?

A simple working definition is: background mental patterns that affect thought, emotion, perception, or behaviour without being noticed immediately. They may include well-practised skills, associations learned from experience, familiar memories, expectations, and responses triggered by recurring situations.

Automatic skillsTyping familiar words, following a known route, or coordinating parts of driving after extensive practice.
Implicit memoryPast experience changing present performance even when I am not deliberately trying to remember that experience.
HabitsA familiar context making a learned response easier to start with little conscious deliberation.
AssociationsA place, sound, expression, or situation quickly activating an expectation or emotional response learned earlier.
Fast judgmentsRapid pattern-based interpretations that occur before slower reflection catches up.

This does not mean that all background activity belongs to one system. Typing, emotional learning, priming, attention, and physiological regulation involve different mechanisms. The label is convenient, but it should not erase those differences.

2. The three-level mind model

The popular model describes the mind as an iceberg with a conscious level above water, a subconscious level just below it, and an unconscious level deep underwater. The image is memorable because it shows that awareness contains only part of ongoing mental activity.

LEVEL 01

Conscious

What I am aware of now: the words I am reading, the sound in the room, the problem I am solving, and the decision I am actively considering.

Example: Choosing what to eat or carefully learning the controls of a car.

LEVEL 02

Subconscious or preconscious

In the popular model, this includes accessible memories, learned habits, familiar beliefs, and automatic skills that are not currently in focus.

Example: Driving a familiar route while attention is directed toward the conversation.

LEVEL 03

Unconscious

Processes outside awareness and, in psychodynamic theory, conflicts, wishes, or repressed material that are not directly accessible but may affect behaviour.

Example: A strong reaction whose source is not immediately understood.

MODERN VIEW

Overlapping processes

Current psychology more often studies awareness, attention, memory, automaticity, learning, and emotion as interacting processes rather than three sealed mental rooms.

Important: A helpful metaphor is not automatically a literal mechanism.

Historical correction: Freud's early topographical model distinguished the conscious, preconscious, and unconscious. The familiar conscious-subconscious-unconscious version is a popular simplification, and the iceberg itself is an illustration associated with the theory rather than a scientific scan of the mind.

3. How does a skill become automatic?

When I first learn a difficult skill, it consumes attention. I must remember instructions, monitor errors, and deliberately select each step. With repeated practice, parts of the sequence can require less conscious control. That frees limited attention for other tasks.

Habit researchers describe a related process: repeating a response in a recurring context strengthens the connection between the context and the response. The cue starts to activate the behaviour more efficiently. That is why a phone notification can trigger checking before a deliberate decision, or why sitting at the same desk can make beginning work easier after a routine is established.

There is no universal number of days. In one frequently cited real-world study, the estimated time to approach maximum automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days across participants and behaviours. Missing one opportunity did not automatically destroy progress. The more responsible lesson is that habit formation varies, and meaningful change is usually measured in weeks or months rather than one dramatic morning.

4. Can changing actions alter automatic thinking?

Yes, within limits. Actions create experiences, and experiences provide information. If I repeatedly avoid speaking because I predict embarrassment, the short-term relief rewards avoidance and I never collect evidence that I could cope. The old belief remains protected from testing.

A small alternative action changes the information available to me. I might ask one prepared question, feel nervous, notice that the moment is survivable, and record what actually happened. One experience may not transform a belief, but repeated experiences can gradually make a different expectation more available. 

Old loop
situation -> automatic prediction -> anxiety -> avoidance
          -> short-term relief -> no new evidence -> prediction stays strong

New loop
situation -> notice prediction -> small chosen action -> discomfort
          -> observe result -> record evidence -> expectation can update

This relationship among situations, thoughts, feelings, bodily responses, and actions is central to cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). CBT is not simply "thinking positively." It involves identifying patterns, examining evidence, testing alternatives, and practising new responses. Different problems require different treatments, and the model does not mean every emotion is chosen or fully controllable.

5. A practical way to influence learned patterns

Step 1: Catch the pattern

When a reaction is strong, pause and record the situation, the first thought or image that appeared, the emotion and body sensations, the urge, and the action that followed. The purpose is observation, not self-criticism. A pattern must become visible before it can be tested.

Step 2: Check the thought

Ask what evidence supports the thought, what evidence does not, whether the wording is absolute, and what I would say to someone else in the same situation. The replacement should be balanced and believable.

Extreme thought

"I always fail, so there is no point starting."

Balanced alternative

"I have struggled with parts of this before. I can test the next small step and learn from the result."

Step 3: Choose a small opposite action

Make the action small enough to repeat. Work for five minutes instead of waiting for motivation. Ask one question instead of trying to become fearless. Take one slow breath before answering instead of demanding perfect calm. The goal is to practise a response, not prove personal worth in one attempt.

Step 4: Attach the action to a clear cue

An implementation intention follows an if-then form: When situation Y occurs, I will do response X. Research across many studies finds that specific contingent plans can help translate goals into action, especially when the goal matters and the plan is rehearsed. 

After I put breakfast away,
I will read for ten minutes at the kitchen table.

When I notice "I cannot do this," 
I will write one more accurate sentence and take the next step.

Step 5: Redesign the environment

Context activates behaviour. Place useful cues close to the desired action and add friction to distractions: put the phone outside the study area, prepare the book before the session, leave walking shoes by the door, or block the distracting site during the chosen work period.

Step 6: Record the result

At the end of the day, write what I did differently and what the experience taught me. This is not a demand to find a positive lesson in everything. It is a way to stop the loudest old prediction from becoming the only evidence I remember.

6. Replacement works better than a mental fight

Trying to force a thought never to appear can make it more noticeable. Research on thought suppression has found both immediate increases and later rebound effects under some conditions. A more useful response is to notice the thought, identify it as a mental event rather than a command, redirect attention, and choose the next helpful action.

Mindfulness practices train present-moment, nonjudgmental awareness and may help people observe automatic thoughts with more distance. The evidence does not support treating mindfulness as a universal cure, and it is not the same as emptying the mind. It is practice in noticing where attention has gone and returning it deliberately.

7. What neuroplasticity does - and does not - mean

Neuroplasticity is the brain's capacity to change structurally and functionally in response to experience, learning, development, injury, and other influences. Learning can strengthen, weaken, or reorganize patterns of neural activity. This helps explain why practice matters.

It does not mean every thought instantly rewires the brain, that positive statements guarantee outcomes, or that someone is responsible for every unwanted feeling. Biological conditions, stress, sleep, development, relationships, environment, and previous learning all influence change. Plasticity makes learning possible; it does not make the brain infinitely or immediately controllable.

8. What will not reliably "reprogram" the subconscious

  • Repeating an unbelievable affirmation while every action continues to support the old pattern.
  • Changing routines randomly without connecting the change to a meaningful goal or stable cue.
  • Expecting one intense day to replace a pattern learned across months or years.
  • Treating every intuition as secret truth rather than a fast judgment that may be useful, biased, or incomplete.
  • Using the idea of the subconscious to blame someone for trauma symptoms, depression, anxiety, compulsions, or other mental-health difficulties.
  • Assuming that insight alone guarantees behaviour change, or that behaviour alone resolves every deeper problem.
When self-help is not enough: Persistent trauma reactions, severe anxiety, depression, compulsions, thoughts of self-harm, or symptoms that disrupt daily life deserve support from a qualified mental-health professional. Habits and journaling can be useful tools, but they are not replacements for appropriate care.

9. A seven-day observation experiment

This is not enough time to create a permanent habit. It is long enough to learn how one pattern operates.

  1. Choose one specific recurring situation, not an entire personality trait.
  2. Write the usual cue, automatic thought, feeling, action, and immediate result.
  3. Create one believable alternative thought.
  4. Choose one action that takes five minutes or less.
  5. Attach it to a stable if-then cue and prepare the environment.
  6. Record what happened without grading the day as a success or failure.
  7. After seven days, ask what cue was reliable, what created friction, what evidence appeared, and what should be adjusted for the next week.

Key takeaways

  • "Subconscious" is a useful everyday label, but automatic processing, implicit memory, habit, and nonconscious processing are more precise scientific terms.
  • The conscious-subconscious-unconscious iceberg is a metaphor, not three separate structures in the brain.
  • Repeated responses in stable contexts can become easier and more automatic over time.
  • Changing an action can create new evidence that gradually influences expectations and learned responses.
  • Balanced thoughts are more useful than extreme negative statements or unbelievable positive affirmations.
  • Clear cues and supportive environments often make repetition easier than relying on willpower alone.
  • Thought suppression can rebound; noticing, reframing, and redirecting are more practical than demanding that a thought disappear.
  • Neuroplasticity supports learning, but it does not promise instant control over thoughts, emotions, or mental-health conditions.
  • My conscious mind can choose and practise a response. Repetition and experience may gradually make that response more automatic.
