Are We Still Dreaming? Awareness, Perception, and What Counts as Real

By Lance AdhikariAugust 8, 202615 min readIndependent research

An Instagram reel asked who is watching a dream, why waking life feels more real, and whether the universe is experiencing itself through us. I wanted to preserve its sense of wonder while checking where neuroscience ends and philosophy begins.

Question I wanted to answer

Does the same awareness experiencing dreams and waking life mean that reality is another dream?

04

My conclusion is no. The comparison reveals something important about first-person experience: both worlds appear within consciousness. It does not prove that they have the same cause, structure, or relationship to an external world.

The reel begins with a powerful question: if my eyes are closed during a dream, what is looking at it? It then says that the same awareness is present while I watch the video, so perhaps waking life is an external projection of consciousness and I am the universe experiencing itself.

I understand why this feels profound. A vivid dream can contain colour, distance, fear, conversation, and a complete environment even though no matching scene is entering the eyes. When I wake, I return to another field of sights, sounds, sensations, and thoughts. In both states, experience is presented from one first-person point of view.

But a good question can be followed by a weak conclusion. To understand the reel carefully, I separated four layers: what I can observe directly, what research can test, what philosophy can interpret, and what remains an unproven metaphysical claim.

1. What is "seeing" a dream?

During a dream, physical eyes are not viewing the imagined room in the way they view a bedroom while awake. Yet the brain can generate visual imagery, voices, movement, emotion, and a sense of presence. Dream research has found that reports of conscious dream experience occur in both rapid-eye-movement and non-REM sleep, and that particular patterns of posterior cortical activity are associated with whether people report having dreamed.

This does not require a tiny observer inside the head watching a private screen. That idea only moves the mystery back one step: who would observe the observer? A more useful scientific description is that interacting brain systems produce a conscious experience with a point of view. The exact explanation of why neural activity is accompanied by subjective experience remains one of the hardest questions in consciousness research.

The first correction: "What sees the dream?" is a meaningful question about consciousness, but the answer is not automatically a separate, faceless entity. Awareness names the fact that experience is occurring; it does not by itself explain the mechanism or prove a spiritual theory.

2. The same capacity, under different conditions

The reel is right in one limited sense: the person who dreams and the person who wakes are not two unrelated experiencers. Memory can connect the states, and both involve the same living organism and its capacity for consciousness. The difference is that the experiences are generated under very different constraints.

Dreaming consciousness

Perception-like experience is largely generated from internal activity. Scenes may shift without warning, impossible events can feel normal, critical judgment is often reduced, and external sensory input has less control over the unfolding world.

Waking consciousness

Experience is continuously constrained by light, sound, touch, body position, movement, memory, and feedback. Objects tend to persist, actions have repeatable consequences, and other observers can independently test many of the same events.

I can look away from a desk and look back to find it in the same place. I can walk around it, touch it, measure it, photograph it, and ask another person to inspect it. None of those tests gives me a view from nowhere, but together they make waking experience more stable, revisable, and publicly checkable than an ordinary dream.

A lucid dream may feel extraordinarily real, and waking perception can be mistaken. The distinction is therefore not "dreams feel fake and waking life always feels correct." It is that waking experience normally participates in a much stronger network of ongoing sensory constraint, cause and effect, memory, action, and shared verification.

3. Perception is constructed - but not arbitrary

The reel also touches a genuine scientific insight: I do not receive reality as a perfect copy. Light reaches the eyes, pressure waves reach the ears, receptors signal touch and body position, and the nervous system integrates those changing signals. Past experience, attention, goals, and expectations help determine what becomes a coherent conscious scene.

Predictive-processing theories describe the brain as using prior models to anticipate the causes of sensory input, then updating those models when prediction and evidence disagree. Researchers debate the details, but the broad lesson is useful: perception is active interpretation, not a camera recording deposited untouched in the mind.

The word constructed is easy to misunderstand. A map is constructed, but that does not mean the territory was invented by the map. A weather model is constructed, but a storm can still overturn its forecast. In the same way, showing that the brain builds an experience does not show that consciousness created every object the experience is about.

External causes + sensory signals + body state
Attention + memory + expectations
Together produce the conscious scene I experience

Constructed experience is not the same as an invented universe.

Some writers use the phrase "controlled hallucination" for perception. It is a striking metaphor, not evidence that waking life is literally a hallucination. The control matters: sensory evidence and action repeatedly correct the brain's expectations.

4. From an observation to a worldview

The reel moves quickly from "all I know appears in consciousness" to "consciousness projects the universe." The first statement is about how knowledge is experienced. The second is a claim about what reality is made of. Philosophy calls that jump a move from epistemology to metaphysics.

Direct observationI have experiences. Dreams can be vivid. I encounter every world from a first-person perspective.
Empirical explanationDreaming and waking correlate with different patterns of brain activity, sensory connection, cognition, and behaviour.
Philosophical interpretationPhysicalism, idealism, non-dualism, panpsychism, and skepticism offer different accounts of mind and world.
Metaphysical certainty"Reality is my dream" or "only consciousness exists" goes beyond what the starting observation establishes.

This does not make metaphysics pointless. Science relies on philosophical assumptions about evidence, explanation, and what counts as a good theory. The lesson is simply to label each level honestly. A worldview can be coherent, meaningful, or spiritually important without becoming a laboratory result.

5. The traditions the reel resembles

The reel blends ideas that sound similar in short-form video but are not interchangeable. Learning the distinctions makes the message much clearer.

VIEW 01

Physicalism or naturalism

Conscious experience depends on physical processes in organisms. The world does not depend on my personal awareness in order to exist, even though all my knowledge of it is mediated by perception.

Reading of the reel: the universe produced organisms capable of observing parts of it.

VIEW 02

Philosophical idealism

Mind, experience, or the mental has a fundamental role in reality. There are several forms, and not all say that one individual person's mind invents the world.

Reading of the reel: experience may be more fundamental than matter.

VIEW 03

Advaita Vedanta

This Indian non-dual tradition argues that the deepest self and ultimate reality, Brahman, are not two. The everyday sense of complete separateness is therefore mistaken.

Reading of the reel: awareness is not merely a private possession of the individual ego.

VIEW 04

Panpsychism and cosmopsychism

Panpsychist views treat mentality as fundamental or widespread; cosmopsychist views begin with a conscious cosmos. Neither automatically says that one individual mind created everyone else.

Reading of the reel: consciousness may belong to reality more deeply than ordinary materialism allows.

VIEW 05

Solipsism

Only my own mind can be known to exist, or in its strongest form only my mind exists. The privacy of experience motivates the problem, but does not prove the conclusion.

Reading of the reel: "nobody else is listening" risks sliding in this direction.

VIEW 06

Buddhist no-self

Many Buddhist traditions analyze the person as changing processes without a permanent, independent self. That is not the same claim as Advaita's identification of the deepest self with ultimate reality.

Important: both can challenge ego-identification while disagreeing about what remains.

6. "You cannot see your face"

From ordinary first-person vision, my face is not positioned in front of my eyes. I can see part of my nose, my hands, and the rest of the world. I encounter my face indirectly through a mirror, camera, touch, or other people's responses.

Phenomenology studies this first-person structure. Experience is organized around a point of view, and the subject is not presented as just another object inside its own visual field. This can loosen the idea that I am only my appearance, name, biography, or current thought.

Still, "my face is not directly visible from here" does not entail "I have no face." The reel turns a feature of perspective into a claim about existence. My body remains part of experience through touch, balance, movement, pain, proprioception, mirrors, records, and interaction with other people.

7. "Nobody else is listening but you"

I have direct access to my own hearing of the reel, not to another viewer's hearing. Even if one million people play the same recording, each experience occurs from its own perspective. This is related to the problem of other minds: I know my own conscious life firsthand, while I infer other minds from speech, behaviour, bodies, shared biology, and interaction.

The inference is not pointless just because it is indirect. Other people surprise me, disagree with me, know things I do not, leave records I did not create, and respond systematically to the world. The existence of independent minds is an extremely strong explanation of that evidence. Personal access to one perspective does not prove that every other perspective is fictional or part of my private consciousness.

A useful distinction: "Only I can directly experience my experience" is a statement about access. "Only I exist" is a statement about reality. The first does not logically establish the second.

8. "The universe is experiencing itself through you"

I can read this sentence in two ways. The poetic, naturalistic version is straightforward: I am made from the same universe I study. Matter formed stars, planets, chemistry, life, nervous systems, and eventually organisms capable of asking what a universe is. Through conscious organisms, parts of nature can model, remember, and reflect on other parts of nature.

The stronger version says that the universe is literally one consciousness looking through every individual. That resembles non-dual, idealist, or cosmopsychist metaphysics. It may be a meaningful worldview, but the dream comparison does not prove it. The sentence is most responsible when treated as poetry or a philosophical possibility rather than a scientific discovery.

9. What the reel gets right - and where it overreaches

What I want to keep

  • Every experience is encountered through consciousness.
  • Dreams show that vivid worlds can be generated without matching current sensory input.
  • My usual identity is something I can observe, not necessarily the whole explanation of awareness.
  • I cannot directly occupy another person's first-person perspective.
  • Ordinary existence becomes remarkable when I stop taking it for granted.

What remains unsupported

  • Dreaming vividly does not prove that waking life is a dream.
  • Constructed perception does not prove consciousness created the external world.
  • Not seeing my face directly does not mean I have no body or identity.
  • Private experience does not prove other minds are versions of me.
  • The existence of the sun and moon does not select one theory of consciousness.

10. A 30-second experiment that does not pretend to prove too much

The reel ends by asking the viewer to turn off the phone and look around. I like the exercise when it is used to sharpen attention rather than settle the nature of the universe.

  1. Put the phone down and let the room become quiet.
  2. Notice colour, brightness, edges, movement, sound, touch, and the position of the body.
  3. Notice thoughts appearing: names for objects, memories, judgments, and the feeling that "I am here."
  4. Separate the observation from the interpretation. "A bright circle is visible" is an observation; "consciousness projected the sun" is an interpretation.
  5. Move, touch an object, or change viewpoint and notice how sensory feedback updates the experience.
  6. Ask which part is directly experienced, which part is inferred, and what evidence would distinguish competing explanations.

The result is not a proof of physicalism, idealism, or non-duality. It is a short encounter with something easy to miss: experience is happening before I finish explaining it. That alone is worth appreciating.

Key takeaways

  • Dreams and waking life are both conscious experiences, but they are generated under importantly different sensory and cognitive conditions.
  • A dream does not require an inner observer watching a screen; "awareness" names the presence of experience without solving how it arises.
  • Waking perception is an active construction shaped by sensory evidence, body signals, memory, attention, and expectation.
  • Constructed perception is not the same as an invented universe. Waking models are repeatedly constrained and corrected by the environment.
  • The continuity of first-person awareness does not prove that waking reality is literally another dream.
  • Idealism, Advaita, panpsychism, cosmopsychism, solipsism, physicalism, and Buddhist no-self are distinct positions, not interchangeable labels.
  • Direct access to my own experience creates the problem of other minds, but it does not prove that other minds do not exist.
  • "The universe experiences itself through us" is defensible as naturalistic poetry; stronger metaphysical versions remain philosophical proposals.
  • The reel succeeds best as a meditation on presence and wonder, not as scientific proof of one final theory of reality.
